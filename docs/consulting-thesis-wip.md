Kyle, I think the uncomfortable truth is this:

**Profusion may be building something people will need, but you are not yet building it around something people viscerally want.**

That distinction is everything.

People need dental floss. They want white teeth, fresh breath, confidence on a date, and not getting shamed by a dentist with a tiny metal hook. People need governance. They want to avoid embarrassment, close a client, survive legal review, publish without fear, win trust, and not have their AI workflow blow up in a screenshot thread on X.

Right now, Profusion is dangerously close to being “AI dental floss.”

Useful. Rational. Defensible. Future-facing. But emotionally cold.

That is probably why your heart is not in it.

The business case you pasted is actually coherent. Annoyingly coherent, even. The wedge is real: messy AI-mediated workflows are creating a new trust gap. Logs are too dumb. GRC platforms are too heavy. C2PA and watermarking address final-artifact provenance but not the upstream human process. A bounded receipt that says “this is what happened, this was reviewed, these are the limits” is a legitimate missing layer.

But here is the issue: **defensibility does not equal desirability.**

A startup can be strategically correct and still spiritually dead to the founder. That is not a small problem. That is the founder equivalent of a check-engine light coming on while you are driving through the desert.

The reason you are doubting Profusion is not because the idea is bad. It is because the current framing lives in the world of liability, caution, auditability, restraint, and institutional anxiety. You, meanwhile, are animated by agency, pedagogy, post-labor capability, AI-augmented human potential, and weirdly beautiful systems that make people more powerful.

Profusion currently says:

“We help organizations prove what happened in high-risk AI workflows.”

That is useful.

But your soul wants to say:

“We help humans and organizations remain trustworthy, capable, and legible in an age where AI can perform, imitate, accelerate, and obscure almost everything.”

That is a much bigger emotional engine.

The strategic wedge can stay narrow. But the founder narrative needs oxygen.

Here is the deeper mismatch I see.

You are trying to sell **governance**, but you are motivated by **human agency**.

You are trying to build **receipts**, but you are intellectually obsessed with **what counts as demonstrated capability in the age of AI**.

You are positioning around **risk containment**, but your best thinking is around **post-labor learning, attention intelligence, and AI-mediated work as a new human frontier**.

That does not mean Profusion is wrong. It means Profusion’s current product language may be trapping your better idea inside compliance cabinetry. Very expensive cabinetry. Probably with a ServiceNow integration and a man named Brad asking about procurement.

The key reframing is this:

**Profusion is not “AI governance software.” Profusion is a trust layer for AI-mediated work.**

That sounds subtle. It is not.

“Governance” makes people think of checklists, legal, compliance, and enterprise bureaucracy.

“Trust layer for AI-mediated work” points toward a broader future: media, hiring, education, contractor evaluation, creative production, agentic workflows, AI-assisted deliverables, and human review. It is still narrow enough to start with receipts, but emotionally and philosophically it connects to what you actually care about.

Now let’s talk about whether people want it.

I do not think people want “workflow evidence receipts.”

I do think certain people urgently want these outcomes:

They want to tell a client, “Yes, we used AI, and here is how we controlled it.”

They want to tell legal, “No, this was not an unreviewed synthetic asset.”

They want to tell a buyer, “Here is the human approval boundary.”

They want to tell themselves, “We are not just winging it with ChatGPT and vibes.”

They want to use AI without feeling exposed.

They want to move faster without looking reckless.

They want to avoid being the person who approved a synthetic asset, AI-generated claim, fake testimonial, likeness-risk video, or candidate evaluation workflow that later becomes a problem.

That is the buyer desire. Not “trust receipts.” The desire is: **permission to move faster without reputational self-immolation.**

There’s your wedge.

The spark may come back if you stop imagining Profusion as a compliance company and start imagining it as a company that gives ambitious operators permission to use powerful AI responsibly.

That is a very different emotional frame.

Not: “We slow you down with governance.”

Instead: “We help you ship AI-mediated work that can survive scrutiny.”

That is much better. It has teeth.

The other reason your heart may not be in it: the customer is still too abstract.

“Agencies, executive communications, compliance-aware content teams” is not yet a customer. That is a slide deck. A customer is:

“Maria, founder of a 12-person video agency, just used AI voiceover and synthetic B-roll for a healthcare client. That person might want Profusion.

Your motivation will likely return only when the buyer becomes human and the product becomes a weapon, not a thesis.

And this is where YC thinking should hit you like a frying pan: you do not need to prove that the future needs Profusion. You need to prove that one stressed person wants a Profusion-style receipt now.

One.

Not a market map. Not a Gartner-shaped hallucination. One human with budget, urgency, embarrassment risk, and a workflow messy enough that your receipt makes them say, “Oh. Yes. I need this.”

Your current danger is that you are using future inevitability to compensate for present emotional ambiguity.

That is common among smart founders. It is also fatal if left untreated.

The phrase “people will need this in the future” can hide three very different realities:

First, “the market is early, but the pain is already forming.” That is good.

Second, “the pain exists, but buyers do not yet know how to name it.” That is workable, but requires founder-led education and sharp category design.

Third, “this is intellectually correct, but nobody wants to buy it until forced by regulation or scandal.” That is dangerous, because then you are building a product for a budget line that does not exist yet.

Profusion needs to determine which one it is.

My honest answer: **do not offer the full sprint pilot for free as your default.**

Offer the conversation for free. Offer a lightweight diagnosis for free. Offer a tiny preview for free. But the actual sprint — where you map a workflow, define evidence boundaries, produce a receipt, and create something usable — should usually be paid.

Not because you are trying to squeeze people. Because payment is the cleanest signal that the problem is real.

Free can create false confidence. People will say yes to free things they do not actually value. That is how founders end up with “positive feedback” and no business. Classic startup purgatory. Warm smiles. Empty Stripe dashboard. Very artisanal suffering.

The more precise principle is this:

**Free discovery. Paid transformation.**

A conversation is free because you are learning and diagnosing.

The sprint is paid because it produces business value.

For Profusion, I would not position the pilot as “try my software.” That sounds premature. I would position it as a contained professional service:

**AI Workflow Evidence Sprint**

“In one week, I’ll help you map one AI-assisted workflow, identify the evidence boundary, capture the key artifacts, document human review, and produce a reviewer-readable receipt your team can use with clients, leadership, or internal review.”

That is not a favor. That is work.

Now, there is one exception. You can do one to three free or near-free pilots, but only as **design partner work**, not charity consulting.

A free pilot should require a clear exchange:

They give you access to a real workflow.

They commit to at least one working session.

They allow you to use an anonymized case study.

They give written feedback.

They agree to a testimonial if the work is useful.

They agree to introduce you to two relevant people if the pilot helps.

They understand that future work is paid.

Without that exchange, free becomes founder self-harm wearing a blazer.

The biggest reason to charge something — even $500 — is that it changes the psychology. A paying customer shows up differently. They respond faster. They give better materials. They take the outcome seriously. They are not merely indulging your interesting idea. They are testing whether your work solves a real problem.

For your first paid pilots, I would use a low-friction founder price:

A **free 20-minute AI workflow risk call**.

Then a **$500–$1,500 evidence sprint** for the first few customers.

Then raise to **$2,500–$5,000** once you have a repeatable process, sample receipts, language, and testimonials.

Do not start at enterprise pricing. Also do not start at “free forever because I’m still validating.” Both are traps. You are not Accenture. You are also not a volunteer librarian for the coming AI governance apocalypse.

The key is to make the first paid step feel small, specific, and low-risk.

Not:

“Would you like to buy Profusion?”

Instead:

“You probably do not need a platform right now. You need one clean evidence packet for one workflow. I can help you produce that in a contained sprint.”

That is consultative. That sounds sane.

How do you get from conversation to pitch?

You do not “switch” from conversation to pitch like a used-car salesman throwing open the trunk. You earn the pitch by diagnosing the problem so clearly that the offer feels like the next logical sentence.

A masterful consultant follows this arc:

First, understand the workflow.

“What are you currently using AI for in client-facing or sensitive work?”

“What happens between the first AI-assisted draft and final approval?”

“Where do the artifacts live?”

“Who reviews it?”

“What would you show a client, lawyer, executive, or reviewer if they asked how AI was used?”

Then, identify the anxiety.

“Has anyone actually asked that yet?”

“Are you worried they will?”

“What would be embarrassing or risky if this workflow were questioned later?”

“What would be hard to reconstruct after the fact?”

Then, expose the gap.

“So right now, the answer exists, but it is scattered across tools and memory.”

That line matters. It is probably one of your strongest sales insights.

Do not say, “You have a compliance problem.”

Say:

“The evidence probably exists, but it is not packaged in a way someone else can review.”

That is sharper. Less threatening. More true.

Then, summarize the pain in their own words.

Something like:

“Here’s what I’m hearing. You are comfortable using AI to move faster, but if a client asked exactly what AI touched, who reviewed it, and what was approved, you would have to reconstruct the answer manually. The risk is not that you are doing something wrong. The risk is that you cannot easily prove what happened.”

That is the bridge.

Then you ask permission to offer.

“Would it be useful if I showed you what a lightweight evidence receipt for one of those workflows could look like?”

They will usually say yes if the pain is real.

Then you pitch the sprint.

“I run a small Evidence Sprint for exactly this. We pick one workflow, map the AI touchpoints, define what the receipt should and should not claim, collect the key artifacts, and produce a one-page reviewer-readable packet. It is not legal certification and it does not magically make anything compliant. It gives you a clear record of what happened, what was reviewed, and where the boundary ends.”

That language is important because it keeps you claims-disciplined. It also makes you sound serious.

Then give the price calmly.

“For the first few design partners, I’m offering this as a founder-led sprint at $750. After that, it will likely move into the $2,500+ range once the process is packaged.”

Do not over-explain the price. Founders often panic after naming a number and start negotiating against themselves like a raccoon trapped in a conference room.

Say the number. Then stop.

If they say, “That sounds interesting,” you tighten.

“Is this something that would be useful for a real workflow you have in the next month, or is it more of a future consideration?”

That question is gold. It separates curiosity from urgency.

If they say future consideration, great. Keep them warm. Do not chase.

If they say real workflow, ask:

“What workflow would you want to use?”

Now you are in sales motion.

Retainer: $1,500–$5,000/month for ongoing governed AI workflow support, depending on client size and cadence.

**Free call → paid sprint → monthly support.**

Do not complicate it with SaaS tiers yet. The SaaS emerges from repeated sprint patterns.

The mark of a good consultant is not “pitching harder.” It is knowing when the problem has become concrete enough to offer help.

You should pitch when three things are true:

They described a real workflow.

They admitted the current process is messy, risky, slow, or hard to explain.

They identified someone who would care about the answer: client, legal, leadership, reviewer, regulator, partner.

No pitch before that. Before that, you are still diagnosing.

The best consultants sell by making the client feel understood before making them feel sold to.

The worst consultants show up with a solution looking for a victim.

You want to sound like this:

You say:

Three: current fit.

You say:

The scope should include:

Problem: what they said.

Workflow: the one process being reviewed.

Deliverables: map, artifact checklist, limitations language, sample receipt.

Timeline: one week or two working sessions.

Boundaries: not legal advice, not compliance certification, not identity verification, not detection.

Price: founder pilot rate.

Next step: payment or signed approval to begin.

Keep it simple. Do not bury them in “platform vision.” The buyer is not purchasing your destiny. They are purchasing relief.
